 ABOUT THIS PIECE
 This is an original pop-art composition created for demonstration purposes —
 Ben-Day dot fields, heavy black contours and flat primaries, in the idiom of
 1960s American pop art. It is not a reproduction of an existing painting, so
 it can be uploaded, stored and displayed freely in your submission.

 BEFORE YOU START — HOW THE TWO IMAGE FIELDS BEHAVE
 An exhibit has both an "Image" upload and an "Image url" text field. The
 application displays Image url first, and only falls back to the upload when
 that is empty. Two consequences worth knowing:

   • In the Django admin, Image url is a required field. You cannot leave it
     blank there, so the uploaded file would never be the one displayed.
     Paste an address instead (one is supplied in step 4).

   • In the React staff screen at <frontend>/manage/new, Image url is
     optional. That is the route to use if you want to demonstrate uploading
     the supplied JPG and seeing it appear on the public page.

 Both routes are valid evidence. Pick whichever the assessment calls for —
 the admin CMS for Persona 2, or the staff screens to show the React path.

Full text:               (the main description on the exhibit page)
Brennan painted Breaker (Noon) at the height of his engagement with
mechanical reproduction, enlarging the halftone dot until the printing
process itself became the subject. The sky is a field of red Ben-Day dots
over cadmium yellow; the sea a single unmodulated plane of cobalt. Between
them runs a hard black horizon, drawn with the ruled confidence of a
commercial illustrator rather than the wrist of a landscape painter.

The breaking wave is the painting's argument. Where a Romantic seascape
would dissolve foam into atmosphere, Brennan gives it a closed black
contour and fills it with dots — spray rendered as newsprint. Critics of
the period read this as parody. Later scholarship has been kinder,
recognising in the work a genuine attempt to find the sublime inside the
vocabulary of mass printing, and noting Brennan's careful, hand-painted
irregularity in every dot.

The work has become the most reproduced of Brennan's marine subjects, and
is widely held to be the finest surviving canvas from his brief and prolific
Bowery period.
